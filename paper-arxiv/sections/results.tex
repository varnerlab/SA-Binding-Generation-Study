We evaluated two forms of training-free conditioning across six protein families
(Table~\ref{tab:cross-family}). Kunitz domains served as the primary test case, and four
additional Pfam seed families---SH3, WW, Homeobox, and Forkhead---tested whether the findings
generalized across family size and PCA geometry. The $\omega$-conotoxin O-superfamily provided
a distinct peptide example associated with the Cav2.2 calcium channel. In every family, we
compared hard curation, which rebuilds the memory from a designated subset, with multiplicity
weighting, which retains the full-family memory but increases the designated patterns' weight.
The subsets were defined by data-selected sequence markers rather than direct activity labels.
Their separation index $S$, which measures how cleanly PCA resolves designated from background
sequences, ranged from \WWSeparation{} for WW to \HomeoboxSeparation{} for Homeobox.

\subsection{Hard Curation Preserves a Selected Marker from Scarce Inputs}

We first asked whether hard curation could transfer a selected marker while preserving the
broader family pattern. Kunitz domains (PF00014; $K=99$, $L=53$ aligned positions) provide an
interpretable test: their P1 residue influences protease specificity, and Lys or Arg at P1 is
associated with trypsin specificity~\cite{laskowskiProteinInhibitors1980}. We designated the
32 K/R-at-P1 sequences as marker-positive and the remaining 67 as marker-negative, while
treating the marker only as a sequence feature rather than an activity measurement.
Separate memories built from these two subsets produced the expected sharp result. All 930
sequences generated from the marker-positive memory carried K/R at P1, and none generated from
the marker-negative memory did; full-family generation produced 38\% K/R compared with 32\% in
the input family. Complete retention is expected because the input subset was selected on the
same marker. It therefore establishes controllability, not independent evidence of trypsin
inhibition.
The conditioning remained localized rather than erasing the family pattern. The top
marker-positive sequences retained all 11 highly conserved positions and all six cysteines
while carrying 19--26 substitutions concentrated at variable sites. Per-position Shannon
entropy correlated with the stored multiple sequence alignment (MSA) for both full-family
generation ($r=0.988$) and marker-positive conditioning ($r=0.932$), with the largest change
at P1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:sequence-analysis}). The result also held when the designated input was
subsampled from 32 sequences down to three: P1 retention remained 100\% at every tested size,
whereas pairwise diversity rose from 0.35 to 0.50 and Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence fell
from 0.070 to 0.009 as more inputs were added (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). Thus, even three inputs
were sufficient to fix the selected marker, but additional inputs improved diversity and
compositional fidelity by defining a broader PCA subspace.

\subsection{Multiplicity Weighting Reveals a Geometry-Dependent Gap}

Hard curation provides a clear endpoint, but it discards the background sequences and changes
the PCA basis. We therefore asked whether multiplicity weighting could tune Kunitz generation
continuously on the full-family basis. The canonical replicated analysis swept $\rho$ from 1
(no bias) to 500; at each value, we recomputed the entropy-crossover temperature
$\beta^{*}(\vr)$ and generated five independent libraries of 620 sequences
(Fig.~\ref{fig:phase-transition}, Table~\ref{tab:per-family-rho}). We then compared the
designated attention mass, $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{des}}$, with the decoded P1 marker fraction,
$f_{\mathrm{obs}}$.
Multiplicity moved the latent mixture as designed, but the decoded sequence composition
responded less strongly. At $\rho=500$, the attention weights assigned
$f_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.996$ to the designated Kunitz patterns, whereas decoded P1~K/R reached
only \KunitzFobsFiveHundred{} $\pm$ \KunitzFobsFiveHundredSD{}. We call this discrepancy
between designated latent mass and the decoded marker marginal the calibration gap
(Eq.~\ref{eq:decoder-pushforward}). Increasing $\rho$ also produced modest global changes:
sequence diversity decreased from 0.56 to 0.51, KL divergence increased from 0.007 to 0.017,
and $\beta^{*}$ shifted from 4.4 at $\rho=1$ ($K_{\mathrm{eff}}=99$) to 9.3 at
$\rho=1{,}000$ ($K_{\mathrm{eff}}=32.1$).

To locate where the marker signal was lost, we followed it through three stages: designated
attention mass $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{des}}$, continuous reconstructed K/R probability
$f_{\mathrm{soft}}$, and decoded K/R fraction $f_{\mathrm{obs}}$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:gap-decomp}). Across 17 values of $\rho$ from 1 to 1{,}000, the attention term
remained close to its target, but $f_{\mathrm{soft}}$ stayed near 0.27 and changed by less than
0.04. The largest loss therefore occurred during PCA reconstruction. Argmax decoding partly
recovered that signal, giving a negative argmax contribution of approximately $-0.25$. Even
when 99.8\% of the attention lay on designated patterns, the leading $d=80$ components did not
cleanly resolve K from G at P1, consistent with the partial PCA overlap measured for Kunitz
($S=\KunitzSeparation$).
An exact-equilibrium comparison ruled out finite-step sampling as the main explanation. At
$\rho\in\{1,10,500\}$, we compared 1{,}640 independent draws from the closed-form Gaussian
mixture with 1{,}640 retained unadjusted Langevin (ULA) states under the same $\beta^{*}$ and
decoder. At $\rho=500$, exact and ULA sampling produced decoded P1~K/R fractions of 0.604 and
0.597, respectively, despite an exact designated-component probability of 0.996; their decoded
amino acid compositions differed by only
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{exact}\parallel\text{ULA})=1.19\times10^{-4}$. Across all three
conditions, basin occupancies differed by at most 0.026 and marker fractions by at most 0.016.
The calibration gap therefore persists at exact equilibrium and arises mainly after latent
sampling, during reconstruction and decoding.

The Kunitz result suggested that conditioning should transfer most effectively when the PCA
representation separates the designated and background subsets. We tested that hypothesis on
four additional Pfam families, using Trp for SH3 (PF00018; $K=55$), a middle-third residue for
WW (PF00397; $K=420$), Gln for Homeobox (PF00046; $K=136$), and His/Asn for Forkhead
(PF00250; $K=246$) as the tracked markers. Together, the five Pfam splits sampled distinct
PCA geometries at comparable conditioning strengths (Table~\ref{tab:per-family-rho}).
The latent weighting remained accurate across the panel: over all six replicated family
sweeps, the largest absolute deviation between designated attention and its target was
\AttnMaxDevPts{} percentage points (\AttnMaxDevFamily{}, $\rho=\AttnMaxDevRho$). What varied
was how much of that latent mass survived decoding. Calibration gaps decreased with separation
overall, although not monotonically, and an exploratory five-family fit gave
$\Delta\approx\RelationIntercept{} \RelationSlope\,S$ with $R^{2}=\RelationRsq$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:separation-vs-gap}). The small panel does not support a universal rule:
leave-one-family-out slopes ranged from \RelationLooSlopeMin{} to \RelationLooSlopeMax{}, and
$R^{2}$ ranged from \RelationLooRsqMin{} to \RelationLooRsqMax{}. We therefore treat the fit
as a preliminary association, with the $\omega$-conotoxin point shown only as an external
comparison. Hard curation, in contrast, retained the selected marker in every family because
its subset-only basis contains no background mass to resolve.

The calibration gap raises a natural comparator question: is the attenuation specific to
SA's PCA decoder? We fitted a multiplicity-weighted profile HMM to each cleaned alignment and
gave it the same labels and $\rho$ grid (Table~\ref{tab:profile-hmm-benchmark}). At
$\rho=500$, the profile HMM recovered the tracked marker at frequencies of 0.988--1.000 in the
five Pfam families, closer than SA to the weighted single-position marginal. This behavior is
expected because a profile HMM estimates match-state emissions directly, whereas SA passes a
latent sample through PCA reconstruction and argmax decoding. The $\omega$-conotoxin HMM
recovered Tyr13 at 0.834 because its weights followed accession designation, which overlaps
with but is not identical to the Tyr13 marker.
More literal marker transfer did not imply a better overall sequence score. In the matched
Kunitz $\rho=500$ comparison, ESM2 assigned pseudo-perplexity
$\SARhoFiveHundredPPL\pm\SARhoFiveHundredPPLSD$ to SA and
$\HMMRhoFiveHundredPPL\pm\HMMRhoFiveHundredPPLSD$ to the weighted profile HMM. Thus the HMM
matched the selected marginal more closely, whereas the SA library received the stronger ESM2
sequence-plausibility score under the same multiplicity ratio.

\subsection{Retrieval Sharpness Trades Marker Recovery against Novelty}

The preceding results separate two controls: $\rho$ sets the intended designated mass, whereas
$\beta/\beta^{*}$ sets retrieval sharpness. Increasing $\beta/\beta^{*}$ from 0.5 to 3.0 at
$\rho=10$, 50, and 200 transferred more Kunitz P1 signal through the PCA representation, but
reduced diversity. At $\rho=200$, for example, P1~K/R rose from 0.61 at $\beta^{*}$ to 0.71
at $3\beta^{*}$ while diversity fell from 0.53 to 0.46 (Supporting Information).
We next removed the background attention entirely and swept $\beta$ to probe the
decode-limited recovery ceiling (Fig.~\ref{fig:mask-betasweep},
Table~\ref{tab:mask-recovery}). This hard mask is the $\rho\rightarrow\infty$ endpoint on the
full-family basis: it sets $\Delta_{\mathrm{attn}}=0$ while leaving PCA reconstruction and
argmax decoding unchanged. Under the mask, P1~K/R increased from 0.46 at $\beta=2$ to 1.00 at
$\beta=512$ without reaching a fixed recovery floor. The gain came at a cost, as novelty fell
from 0.42 at the mask's $\beta^{*}$ to 0.16 at $\beta=512$.
Matched-$\beta$ comparisons led to the same conclusion. The mask exceeded finite-$\rho$
weighting by 0.085 (SE 0.028) at $f_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.5$ and 0.053 (SE 0.029) at
$f_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.7$, but by only 0.003 (SE 0.030) at $f_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.99$, where the
intended masses nearly coincide. Notably, a 0.5 change in intended mass produced only a 0.085
change in decoded marker fraction, illustrating again how strongly the latent-to-sequence map
attenuates the response. Hard curation avoided that attenuation at low $\beta$ and achieved
complete marker retention with novelty 0.32 because it rebuilt the PCA basis from the
designated subset. Together, these experiments show that sharper retrieval can compensate for
decoder loss, but only by sacrificing novelty; rebuilding the representation is more direct
when strict marker retention is required.

\subsection{Curated Conditioning Transfers Conotoxin Sequence Features}

Having established the conditioning mechanics on Pfam domains, we asked whether hard curation
could transfer features in a distinct disulfide-rich peptide family. $\omega$-Conotoxins block
voltage-gated calcium channels, including Cav2.2~\cite{omegaConotoxinSAR2006}; the family
includes MVIIA (ziconotide), an approved non-opioid analgesic~\cite{ziconotideReview2004}.
Tyr13 is a reported Cav2.2 marker whose hydroxyl group contacts the channel pore, while basic
residues elsewhere in the peptide contribute to electrostatic complementarity.
We aligned 74 SwissProt $\omega$-conotoxins and retained 26 positions after gap filtering.
The curated input list designated 23 accessions, with the other 51 used as background but not
asserted to be nonbinders. Importantly, accession designation and Tyr13 were related but
distinct labels: they agreed for 64 of 74 sequences (86.5\%), with 19 Tyr13-positive sequences
in the designated set and six in the background. We therefore report accession conditioning
and Tyr13 recovery separately; an accession-level audit identifies which designations have
repository activity metadata and which are supported only by the input list.
Hard curation again shifted the selected sequence statistics. From 1{,}550 generated sequences
per condition, Tyr occurred at the marker position in 46.9\% of full-family generation versus
33.8\% of its input, and in 98.3\% of designated-subset generation versus 82.6\% of its input
(Table~\ref{tab:conotoxin-pharmacophore}). Designated-subset generation also contained 20.1\%
Lys or Arg, close to 19.7\% in its input and above the 12.0\% in full-family generation.

The broader sequence pattern was retained alongside these marker shifts. Frequency heatmaps
preserved Tyr13, the cysteine framework, and variable-loop diversity
(Fig.~\ref{fig:conotoxin-loop}); per-position entropy correlations were $r=0.745$ for the
designated subset and $r=0.997$ for the full family. The five highest-pLDDT designated-subset
sequences retained all six highly conserved positions and all six cysteines while introducing
5--11 substitutions at variable sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:conotoxin-sequence-analysis}).
Comparison with 12 published structure--activity relationship positions gave a consistent but
strictly sequence-level result. Designated-subset generation retained Tyr13 at 98.3\% and the
six invariant disulfide-forming cysteines at frequencies of at least 97.4\%. It also increased
K/R at reported interaction positions, including Arg10 (82.3\% versus 69.6\% in the designated
input) and position~21 (66.2\% versus 47.8\%). These enrichments agree with residues highlighted
by prior mutagenesis, but they do not test mutation effects in the generated
backgrounds~\cite{kimTyr13Essential1995,satoBasicResidues1993,lewisConotoxinSAR2012}. The top
three sequences nevertheless had predicted disulfide-constrained loop architectures consistent
with an inhibitor cystine-knot-like fold (Fig.~\ref{fig:fold-superposition}). Thus, without
retraining or a Cav2.2 target structure, hard curation transferred the designated accessions'
sequence statistics into a new library; it did not establish target binding.

\subsection{Generated Sequences Retain Plausibility and Fold Similarity}

Finally, we asked whether marker control came at the expense of global sequence or structural
plausibility. For 50 Kunitz sequences from each source---stored, full-family SA,
marker-positive SA, marker-negative SA, and profile-HMM emission---ESMFold assigned similar
model scores to SA and stored sequences (Table~\ref{tab:structure-validation}). Mean
pLDDT was $90.4\pm1.2$ for full-family SA, $90.7\pm1.1$ for marker-positive SA, and
$89.3\pm3.2$ for stored sequences, with every SA sequence above 70. Corresponding TM-scores
against the experimental Kunitz structure (1BPI, chain~A) were $0.84\pm0.01$,
$0.83\pm0.01$, and $0.83\pm0.03$. HMM-emitted sequences scored lower and more variably, with
mean pLDDT $60.4\pm13.1$, 20\% above 70, and mean TM-score $0.60\pm0.19$.
Independent AlphaFold2-ptm predictions gave the same qualitative picture: Kunitz TM-scores
were 0.83--0.85 at pLDDT 93--95, whereas the shorter $\omega$-conotoxins had TM-scores of
0.35--0.47 at pLDDT 68--79 (Table~\ref{tab:af2-validation})
~\cite{jumperHighlyAccurate2021,mirdita2022colabfold}. ESM2 sequence scoring also placed SA
near the stored family distribution. Pseudo-perplexity was $4.78\pm0.64$ for full-family SA,
$4.72\pm0.60$ for marker-positive SA, and $4.97\pm0.98$ for stored sequences, compared with
$16.1\pm4.2$ for HMM emission.
A separate unconditional Kunitz benchmark confirmed the distinction among generation,
conditioning, and resampling. Across five HMMER3 emissions and five bootstrap
resamples~\cite{hmmer3},
full-family SA gave a P1~K/R fraction of $0.38\pm0.03$ and KL divergence
$0.0076\pm0.0009$, compared with $0.17\pm0.04$ and $0.027\pm0.002$ for HMM emission.
Bootstrap resampling matched composition most closely (KL $\approx0.001$) but produced no novel
sequences. Marker-positive SA, by contrast, reached P1~K/R $1.0\pm0.0$, a conditioning control
that neither unconditional baseline provides (Table~\ref{tab:structure-validation}). These
model-based evaluations do not demonstrate function, but they show that SA's marker control
was compatible with family-level sequence plausibility and Kunitz-like predicted folds.
