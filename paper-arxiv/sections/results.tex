We evaluated SA on six protein families spanning a range of sizes, functions, and PCA-space
geometries (Table~\ref{tab:cross-family}). Five were Pfam seed families, Kunitz
(PF00014, $K=99$), SH3 (PF00018, $K=55$), WW (PF00397, $K=420$), Homeobox
(PF00046, $K=136$), and Forkhead (PF00250, $K=246$), which we used to test both hard
curation and multiplicity-weighted conditioning. Their designated subsets were defined by
data-selected single-position markers whose residue classes were motivated by prior literature
where available, though the markers are not themselves direct activity labels. The sixth
family was the $\omega$-conotoxin O-superfamily ($K=74$, curated from SwissProt), included as
a peptide example associated with the Cav2.2 calcium channel. For each family we split the
sequences into designated and background subsets, built the conditioned memory matrices, and
generated sequences at the entropy-crossover inverse temperature $\beta^{*}$. We characterized
the resulting geometry by the separation index $S$, which measures how well PCA separates the
two subsets and which ranged from \WWSeparation{} for WW to \HomeoboxSeparation{} for
Homeobox, a range that let us compare conditioning across geometries differing in how cleanly
the designated subset is resolved.

We restricted the memory matrix to a K/R-at-P1-positive subset of Kunitz domains
(PF00014; $K=99$ sequences, $L=53$ aligned positions) and tested whether the selected
sequence marker transferred to generated sequences (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). Kunitz domains
are serine protease inhibitors whose P1 residue at the primary contact site influences target
specificity, and Lys or Arg at P1 is associated with trypsin
specificity~\cite{laskowskiProteinInhibitors1980}, although that association is not by itself
an activity measurement for every sequence carrying the residue. We therefore designated the
32 sequences with K/R at P1 as the marker-positive subset ($K_{\mathrm{des}} = 32$) and the
remaining 67 as marker-negative background ($K_{\mathrm{bg}} = 67$). We built a separate
memory matrix for each group, selected $\beta^{*}$ from the entropy-crossover onset, and
generated 930 sequences per condition from 30 chains run for 5{,}000 steps and thinned after
burn-in. Every designated-subset-conditioned sequence carried K/R at P1 and no
background-conditioned sequence did, while full-family generation produced 38\% K/R against
32\% in the input family. Complete K/R retention is expected because the designated input was
selected on this marker, so it records marker retention rather than independent evidence of
trypsin inhibition. Sequence-level inspection showed that the top
K/R-positive-subset-conditioned sequences preserved all 11 highly conserved positions, all six
cysteines, and K/R at P1 while carrying 19--26 substitutions concentrated at variable sites.
Per-position Shannon entropy correlated with the stored MSA for both full-family generation
($r = 0.988$) and K/R-positive-subset conditioning ($r = 0.932$), with the largest change at
P1, where conditioning restricted the residues to K/R (Fig.~\ref{fig:sequence-analysis}).

We next varied the designated input size, sampling
$K_{\mathrm{des}} \in \{3, 5, 8, 10, 15, 20, 25, 32\}$ Kunitz K/R-positive sequences with
three replicates per size (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). P1 marker retention was 100\% at every
size, including $K_{\mathrm{des}}=3$, while pairwise sequence diversity increased from 0.35 at
$K_{\mathrm{des}}=3$ to 0.50 at $K_{\mathrm{des}}=32$ and KL divergence decreased from 0.070
to 0.009 over the same range. Smaller input sets define a narrower PCA subspace and therefore
produced less diverse libraries, so marker retention held from designated inputs as small as
three sequences while compositional fidelity and diversity both improved as inputs were added.

We tested multiplicity-weighted generation on the Kunitz family ($K = 99$,
$K_{\mathrm{des}} = 32$ K/R-positive sequences, $K_{\mathrm{bg}} = 67$ background),
sweeping $\rho$ from 1 (standard SA, no bias) to 500 in the canonical replicated
analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase-transition}, Table~\ref{tab:per-family-rho}). For each $\rho$ we
computed the multiplicity vector $\vr$, found $\beta^{*}(\vr)$ from the entropy-crossover
onset of the weighted attention distribution, and ran five independent replicates of 20 chains
with 620 retained samples per replicate. At each $\rho$ we recorded both the mean attention
weight on designated patterns, $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{des}}$, and the decoded K/R fraction at P1,
$f_{\mathrm{obs}}$. Across all six canonical replicated sweeps the largest absolute attention
deviation was \AttnMaxDevPts{} percentage points
(\AttnMaxDevFamily{}, $\rho=\AttnMaxDevRho$), close but not exact finite-sample agreement with
the target mixture fraction. The decoded marker fraction did not follow. For Kunitz, P1~K/R
reached \KunitzFobsFiveHundred{} $\pm$ \KunitzFobsFiveHundredSD{} at $\rho=500$, short of the
$f_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.996$ that the attention weights achieved. This discrepancy
between latent designated mass and the decoded marker marginal is the calibration gap, and it
reflects both false negatives and false positives under the complete latent-to-sequence map
(Eq.~\ref{eq:decoder-pushforward}). Over the same range sequence diversity decreased from 0.56
to 0.51 and KL divergence increased from 0.007 to 0.017, while the entropy crossover
$\beta^{*}$ shifted from 4.4 at $\rho=1$ ($K_{\mathrm{eff}} = 99$) to 9.3 at
$\rho=1{,}000$ ($K_{\mathrm{eff}} = 32.1$), displacing the entropy curves to the right as
$\rho$ increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase-transition}).

To examine this gap, we measured three layers of the signal chain
(Eq.~\ref{eq:gap-decomp}) over 17 values of $\rho$ from 1 to 1{,}000 with five replicates per
value, recording designated attention mass $\bar{a}_{\mathrm{des}}$, continuous reconstructed
K/R probability $f_{\mathrm{soft}}$, and decoded K/R fraction $f_{\mathrm{obs}}$. The
attention gap remained small. The PCA term was the largest component, since
$f_{\mathrm{soft}}$ stayed near 0.27 and varied by less than 0.04 over the full range of
$\rho$. The argmax term was negative ($\approx-0.25$), so discrete decoding recovered some of
the signal lost during PCA reconstruction. Even with 99.8\% of the attention on designated
patterns the continuous reconstruction did not resolve K from G at P1, because that variation
was not well represented by the leading $d=80$ principal components, consistent with the
Kunitz split's separation index of $S=\KunitzSeparation$, which indicates partial PCA overlap.

The closed-form Gaussian-mixture target permits independent equilibrium sampling without a
Markov chain, so we compared 1{,}640 exact draws with 1{,}640 retained ULA states at
$\rho\in\{1,10,500\}$ using the same $\beta^{*}$ and decoder in each comparison, with the full
comparison provided in the Supporting Information. At $\rho=500$ the exact
designated-component probability was $f_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.996$, while the decoded P1~K/R
fraction was 0.604 under exact sampling and 0.597 under ULA, and the amino acid composition
divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{exact} \parallel \text{ULA})$ between the two decoded
libraries was $1.19\times10^{-4}$. Across all three conditions, exact and ULA MAP-designated
basin occupancies differed by at most 0.026 and the decoded P1~K/R fractions by at most 0.016.
The calibration gap therefore persists at exact equilibrium. Finite-step ULA discretization
and mixing error were not its main source.

We repeated the multiplicity analysis on four additional Pfam families and found that the
calibration gap tracked PCA separation across the panel
(Fig.~\ref{fig:separation-vs-gap}, Table~\ref{tab:cross-family}). Each split was defined by a
data-selected marker, Trp for SH3 (PF00018; $K=55$), a middle-third residue for WW
(PF00397; $K=420$), Gln for Homeobox (PF00046; $K=136$), and His/Asn for Forkhead
(PF00250; $K=246$), so that together with Kunitz the five families sampled distinct PCA-space
geometries at comparable conditioning strengths (Table~\ref{tab:per-family-rho}). Their
calibration gaps varied inversely with separation overall, though not monotonically, and an
exploratory unweighted fit across the five families yielded
$\Delta \approx \RelationIntercept{} \RelationSlope\,S$ with $R^{2}=\RelationRsq$. That fit
was sensitive to which family was included, since leave-one-family-out slopes ranged from
\RelationLooSlopeMin{} to \RelationLooSlopeMax{} and $R^{2}$ from
\RelationLooRsqMin{} to \RelationLooRsqMax{}. The $\omega$-conotoxin point was displayed as an
external comparison rather than folded into the Pfam fit, so these data establish a
preliminary association rather than a prospective threshold or decision rule. The attention
weights nonetheless tracked $f_{\mathrm{eff}}$ closely in all six families, indicating that
the multiplicity-weighted energy reached its target latent mass regardless of how much of that
mass survived decoding. Hard curation, by contrast, achieved 100\% marker retention in every
family independent of the separation index, which is expected because its PCA basis contains
only the designated subset and therefore has no background mass to resolve.

We also tested whether increasing $\beta$ beyond $\beta^{*}$ transferred more marker signal
through the PCA representation, sweeping $\beta/\beta^{*}$ from 0.5 to 3.0 at
$\rho = 10$, 50, and 200 on the Kunitz domain (Supporting Information). Higher $\beta$
increased the P1~K/R fraction and reduced diversity, so that at $\rho = 200$ and
$\beta = 3\beta^{*}$ P1~K/R reached 0.71 against 0.61 at $\beta^{*}$ while diversity decreased
from 0.53 to 0.46. Conditioning strength $\rho$ and retrieval sharpness $\beta/\beta^{*}$
therefore provide separate controls over marker retention and diversity, which raised the
question of how much marker signal the decoder can transfer once the background contribution
is removed entirely.

We next held the background attention mass at zero and swept $\beta$ to measure the
decode-limited recovery ceiling
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mask-betasweep}, Table~\ref{tab:mask-recovery}). Hard attention masking is the
$\rho \to \infty$ endpoint of the multiplicity axis on the full-family basis, and because it
removes the background contribution exactly it gives $\Delta_{\mathrm{attn}} = 0$ in
Eq.~\ref{eq:gap-decomp}, leaving the PCA and argmax terms as the only remaining sources of
loss. Under the fixed mask the P1~K/R fraction increased from 0.46 at $\beta = 2$ to 1.00 at
$\beta = 512$ without reaching a plateau, so the residual decode gap
$\Delta_{\mathrm{PCA}} + \Delta_{\mathrm{argmax}}$ shrank as retrieval sharpened rather than
saturating at a fixed floor. At matched $\beta$, hard masking recovered more marker signal
than finite-$\rho$ weighting, by 0.085 (SE 0.028) at $f_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.5$ and 0.053
(SE 0.029) at $f_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.7$, and that advantage fell to 0.003 (SE 0.030) at
$f_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.99$, where the mask and the strongest finite-$\rho$ condition coincided.

Because hard masking removes the background components and raises the intended designated mass
from $f_{\mathrm{eff}}$ to one, matched-$\beta$ comparisons carry different intended masses,
and their difference should shrink as $f_{\mathrm{eff}}$ approaches one. Attention realized
the intended mass in each condition to within a fraction of a percent
($\Delta_{\mathrm{attn}} \approx 0$), yet a 0.5 difference in intended mass produced only a
0.085 difference (SE 0.028) in observed marker fraction, because the latent-to-sequence map
attenuated the response through noise, PCA reconstruction, and argmax decoding. Both retrieval
sharpness $\beta$ and conditioning strength $\rho$ therefore affected recovery, and sharpening
retrieval carried a cost, since novelty decreased from 0.42 at the mask's $\beta^{*}$ to 0.16
at $\beta = 512$ (Table~\ref{tab:mask-recovery}). Hard curation reached complete marker
retention at low $\beta$ with novelty of 0.32, because its subset-only basis retains
designated variation that the leading full-family components blur.

We used the $\omega$-conotoxin O-superfamily to test curated conditioning beyond classical
protein domains (Table~\ref{tab:conotoxin-pharmacophore}, Fig.~\ref{fig:conotoxin-loop}).
These peptides come from cone snails, are rich in disulfide bonds, and block voltage-gated
calcium channels including Cav2.2 in sensory neurons~\cite{omegaConotoxinSAR2006}, which
contributes to pain signaling between peripheral nociceptors and the spinal cord. The family
includes $\omega$-conotoxin MVIIA (ziconotide), an approved non-opioid analgesic for
intractable pain~\cite{ziconotideReview2004}, and prior modeling of ATP-gated calcium dynamics
in dorsal root ganglion neurons also identified Cav2.2 as part of the pain signaling
network~\cite{songModelingAnalysis2009}. Within this family, Tyr13 is a reported Cav2.2 marker
in MVIIA numbering whose hydroxyl group contacts the channel pore and whose substitution
abolishes binding, while basic residues distributed across the peptide contribute to
electrostatic complementarity with the channel entrance. We compiled 74 SwissProt
$\omega$-conotoxin sequences, aligned them with MAFFT~\cite{mafft2013}, and retained 26
positions after gap filtering, with the study's curated Cav2.2-associated input list
designating 23 accessions and the remaining 51 sequences forming a background set for
conditioning that is not asserted to be nonbinders. An accession-level audit records which
designations have activity metadata in the repository and which are supported only by the
input list.

We ran two SA experiments with 50 chains, 5\,000 steps, and $\alpha = 0.01$, generating
1\,550 sequences per condition, one using the full family ($K = 74$, $\beta^{*} = 3.23$) and
the other using only the designated accessions ($K = 23$, $\beta^{*} = 2.72$). Tyr occurred at
the marker position in 33.8\% of the full-family input and 46.9\% of full-family generation,
and in 82.6\% of the designated input and 98.3\% of designated-subset generation
(Table~\ref{tab:conotoxin-pharmacophore}). Accession designation and Tyr13 are distinct
labels that agree for 64 of 74 stored sequences (86.5\%), with 19 marker-positive sequences in
the designated set and six in the background, so we report designation conditioning and Tyr13
recovery separately. Designated-subset generation contained 20.1\% Lys or Arg against 19.7\%
in its input, whereas full-family generation contained 12.0\%, consistent with the lower K/R
content of the full-family input.

Per-position frequency heatmaps were similar for the designated input and its generated
sequences (Fig.~\ref{fig:conotoxin-loop}). Tyr13, the conserved Cys framework, and
variable-loop diversity were all retained, and the residual heatmaps showed small changes
across positions. Sequence-level analysis gave the same pattern
(Fig.~\ref{fig:conotoxin-sequence-analysis}), with the five highest-pLDDT sequences retaining
all six highly conserved positions, all six cysteines, and Tyr at the marker while carrying
5--11 substitutions at variable sites. The entropy correlation was $r = 0.745$ for
designated-subset generation and $r = 0.997$ for full-family generation, the lower
designated-subset value reflecting reduced variation at co-varying loop positions. Comparing
12 positions with published structure--activity relationship observations
(Table~\ref{tab:sar-agreement}), designated-subset generation retained Tyr13 at 98.3\% and all
six disulfide-forming cysteines at frequencies of at least 97.4\%, which is unsurprising given
that those cysteines are invariant in the designated input. It also raised the frequency of
basic residues at loop~2 positions reported as important for channel interaction (Arg10:
82.3\% K/R against 69.6\% in the designated input) and at position~21 (66.2\% K/R against
47.8\%), exceeding full-family generation at every reported position outside the disulfide
framework, where both conditions sat at or near 1.00. The generated library therefore enriched
several residues reported by prior mutagenesis, though these frequencies do not test mutation
effects in the generated
backgrounds~\cite{kimTyr13Essential1995,satoBasicResidues1993,lewisConotoxinSAR2012}. The top
three designated-subset sequences had predicted disulfide-constrained loop architectures
consistent with an inhibitor cystine-knot-like fold (Fig.~\ref{fig:fold-superposition}), so
curated conditioning transferred sequence statistics from the 23 designated accessions to the
generated library without retraining and without the Cav2.2 target structure.

We compared complex-level predictions for SA-generated and natural $\omega$-conotoxins using
ColabFold AlphaFold2-multimer with the Cav2.2 pore-domain target
(Table~\ref{tab:docking-validation}). The SA designated-subset group ($n=10$) gave pTM
$0.221\pm0.007$, iPTM $0.105\pm0.008$, and interface pLDDT $37.2\pm5.4$, the SA full-family
group ($n=10$) gave $0.219\pm0.008$, $0.104\pm0.007$, and $38.2\pm4.0$, and the natural
controls ($n=5$) gave $0.228\pm0.004$, $0.108\pm0.007$, and $40.7\pm3.5$. All three groups
received low-confidence complex predictions. Exact permutation tests detected no group
difference on the four reported metrics (all $p>0.05$), but those tests do not establish
equivalence or preserved binding geometry, so experimental complex structures and binding
assays are required to evaluate Cav2.2 interaction.

We predicted folds for 50 sequences from each source (stored, full-family SA,
K/R-positive SA, K/R-negative SA, and HMM baseline) using ESMFold and computed TM-scores
against the experimental Kunitz domain structure (1BPI, chain~A) to compare model-predicted
structure summaries (Table~\ref{tab:structure-validation}). SA-generated and stored sequences
received similar model scores, with mean pLDDT of $90.4 \pm 1.2$ for SA full-family,
$90.7 \pm 1.1$ for SA K/R-positive, and $89.3 \pm 3.2$ for stored sequences, and every SA
sequence exceeded the pLDDT $>70$ threshold. Their mean TM-scores were $0.84 \pm 0.01$,
$0.83 \pm 0.01$, and $0.83 \pm 0.03$, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:fold-superposition}),
whereas HMM-emitted sequences scored lower and more variably, at mean pLDDT $60.4 \pm 13.1$
with 20\% above the threshold and mean TM-score $0.60 \pm 0.19$. AlphaFold2-ptm predictions
via ColabFold gave the same picture, with Kunitz TM-scores of $0.83$--$0.85$ at pLDDT
$93$--$95$ and the shorter $\omega$-conotoxins at TM-scores of $0.35$--$0.47$ and pLDDT
$68$--$79$ (Table~\ref{tab:af2-validation})~\cite{jumperHighlyAccurate2021,mirdita2022colabfold}.
Sequence plausibility, assessed by ESM2-650M masked marginal scoring, followed the same
ordering, with pseudo-perplexity of $4.78 \pm 0.64$ for SA full-family, $4.72 \pm 0.60$ for SA
K/R-positive, and $4.97 \pm 0.98$ for stored sequences against $16.1 \pm 4.2$ for HMM-emitted
sequences, the lower SA values being consistent with generation near the center of the family
distribution. We also compared SA directly against five HMMER3 profile-HMM emissions of 150
sequences and five bootstrap resamples~\cite{hmmer3}, of which the seed-42 emission was also
used for the model-based evaluations. The same separation appeared in marker terms, since
full-family SA gave a P1~K/R fraction of $0.38 \pm 0.03$ and KL divergence of
$0.0076 \pm 0.0009$ while HMM emission gave $0.17 \pm 0.04$ and $0.027 \pm 0.002$.
Bootstrap resampling preserved
composition (KL $\approx 0.001$) but produced no novel sequences, and K/R-positive-subset SA
reached a P1~K/R fraction of $1.0 \pm 0.0$, a marker-conditioning control that neither
unconditional baseline provides (Table~\ref{tab:structure-validation}).
